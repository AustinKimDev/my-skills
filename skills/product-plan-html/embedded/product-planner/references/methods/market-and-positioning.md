# Market and Positioning

## SWOT

Separate internal strengths/weaknesses from external opportunities/threats relative to a stated objective. Attach evidence or mark each item as a hypothesis. Translate the relevant combinations into strategic options or risks. A four-cell list alone is not a strategy or market validation.

## 3C

Compare Customer, Competitor and Company: needs/contexts and alternatives; competing ways of solving the problem; actual capabilities/constraints. Include substitutes where relevant. Record source dates and distinguish observed competitor behavior from inferred intent. Derive opportunities and questions, not unsupported market-fit claims.

## STP

Segment by meaningful differences in needs or behavior, evaluate candidate targets against evidence and ability to serve, then propose positioning for the selected target relative to alternatives. Explain whom the proposal excludes and why. Do not invent segment sizes or treat demographic stereotypes as validated needs.

Output a focused comparison, recommended direction, assumptions and next evidence needed. None of these methods requires a new market study for a bounded component fix.

## Worked application: a room-booking product

All inputs below are fictional hypotheses, not market research. Question: should a room-booking product focus first on small self-managed teams or complex centrally managed organizations?

### 3C establishes what needs evidence

- Customer: hypothesize that small teams struggle with reservation changes and larger organizations need layered approvals. Needed evidence: actual workflows, frequency, consequences and workarounds from each candidate group.
- Competitor: a shared calendar is a possible substitute; do not claim named competitors lack a feature. Inspect actual alternatives and what users consider adequate.
- Company: assume an existing simple booking flow for the exercise. Administrative capacity, integrations and cost are unknown, so capability-based conclusions remain conditional.

### SWOT turns observations into options

Given those fictional assumptions, “simple existing flow” is a possible internal strength and “unproven complex approval capability” an internal weakness. “Unmet self-service needs” is an external opportunity hypothesis; “current calendar is good enough” an external threat hypothesis.

Interpretation: investigate lightweight self-service cancellation before expanding approval machinery. This is a candidate direction supported by assumptions, not proof of demand. If calendars already solve the problem well, the option may be rejected rather than rescued by extra features.

### STP makes the scope explicit

Segment by workflow and permission complexity. Conditionally target small self-managed teams if evidence supports frequent unresolved reservation-change friction and ability to serve them. Proposed positioning: clear booking status and predictable self-service changes. Exclude multi-level approvals from the initial scope unless that evidence changes the target.

Output: two target options, a conditional recommendation, excluded needs, and evidence that would reverse the choice. Connect the selected target to actor/policy requirements; for example [reservation cancellation](../examples/reservation-cancellation.md) explores a simple self-service direction. Do not manufacture segment size, willingness to pay, competitor superiority or a market-fit verdict.
